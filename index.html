Tooth Be Told

Make the call, then see what the experts chose.

How does one new finding change your decision? Clinical judgement is rarely about a single right answer. It is about how well you update a decision when new information arrives. Compare your thinking with a panel of experienced clinicians across three short dental cases.

  • 3cases
  • 9items
  • about 15minutes
  • not graded

How to use this tool

  1. Read the case recordEach item shows a short patient record. Treat everything in it as true, and assume nothing beyond it.
  2. Consider the optionYou are given one option you might be thinking of, such as a diagnosis, an investigation or a treatment.
  3. Judge the new findingDecide how the new finding changes that option, from -2 to +2. Judge only the effect of that finding on that option, not the whole case. Add a sentence of reasoning, then press Lock in my answer.
  4. Compare with the panelSee how ten experienced clinicians responded and why, then move to the next item. At the end you get a summary you can copy and share with your tutor.

Reading the scale

  • -2Much less. The finding makes the option clearly unsuitable or unlikely.
  • -1Less. The finding weakens the option, but it remains possible.
  • 0Not affected. The finding is irrelevant to this option. It does not mean "I do not know".
  • +1More. The finding supports the option.
  • +2Much more. The finding makes the option clearly the right direction.

A worked example

If you were thinking ofTaking a new set of bitewing radiographs for an adult at a first visit
and then you findShe has brought bitewings taken at another practice last month.
this option becomesMuch less useful (-2)

Why -2? The images you wanted already exist, so repeating them adds radiation without adding information. A clinician who also wanted to check the image quality might choose -1. That is why a panel is used: reasonable experts can differ slightly, and the credit you receive reflects how close you are to the group.

Good to know

  • There is no single correct answer. Credit reflects how many panel members agreed with you, so a response chosen by some of the panel still earns partial credit.
  • Trust your first judgement, then write your reasoning. The note is for you and is shown again in your summary.
  • You cannot change an answer after locking it in. The panel appears only after you commit, so the comparison stays honest.
  • Plan for 1 to 2 minutes per item. There are 9 items in 3 cases.
  • Nothing is saved or graded. Your answers stay in this browser tab and are lost if you refresh, so copy your summary at the end if you want to keep it.
Note for educators

This is a prototype. The three cases and the panel responses are illustrative examples written to demonstrate the format, and they have not been validated. Before using it with students, replace them with your own cases and responses from a real expert panel. Everything sits in a single data block at the top of the page script.

Credit per item follows the aggregate scoring method used for script concordance tests: the number of panel members who chose the student's answer, divided by the number who chose the most popular answer. Answers stay in the student's browser and are not collected.

How to answer this item

Choose how that finding changes the option: -2 or -1 if it makes the option less likely, useful or appropriate, 0 if it is irrelevant, +1 or +2 if it makes the option more so. Questions for reflection

  • Which item did you find hardest to settle? What information would have helped you decide?
  • Where you differed from the panel, was your reasoning weaker, or did you weigh a different consideration, such as the patient's circumstances?
  • Where the panel itself was split, what does that tell you about the limits of a single right answer?
